\begin{abstract}
Face recognition models are no longer judged only by clean verification:
black-box attacks, common corruptions, and broader robustness benchmarks show
that deployed recognizers can fail under several kinds of shift and adversarial
pressure. At the same time, robust learning has already used model or attack
ensembles to reduce overfitting to a single adversary, and face recognition has
developed several strong margin-based recognizer objectives. This work studies
the intersection of these ideas: after introducing attacker diversity, does
adding recognizer-objective diversity make adversarial training more rounded
for one deployed target recognizer? The method treats robustness as a
training-time pressure problem: a single ArcFace/iResNet100 target is trained
on WebFace4M while adversarial examples are generated by multiple attack
families and, in the full setting, also judged by external ArcFace, CosFace,
and CurricularFace recognizer heads. The experiments are organized as an
ablation from clean training to recognizer-only pressure, single-attack
training, multi-attack training, and finally the full recognizer-plus-attacker
setting. This separates the benefit of attack diversity from the additional
benefit of recognizer diversity: the central comparison holds PGD, BPFA, and
DFANet fixed, then adds the external recognizer heads. Adding this recognizer
pressure raises average robust accuracy from 52.32\% to 56.55\% and reduces
average attack success from 44.32\% to 39.45\%, but verification accuracy
falls from 98.33\% to 98.21\% and EER rises from 1.78\% to 1.93\%. Inference
cost is unchanged because only the target recognizer is deployed. The result
is a calibrated tradeoff: recognizer diversity improves measured digital
robustness beyond attack diversity alone, but shifts the model along a
robustness--verification--training-cost frontier.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Face recognition (FR) systems are normally evaluated through clean
identification or verification accuracy, but deployment failures often come
from distribution shift and deliberate adversaries. Black-box attacks have
been demonstrated against FR systems \cite{dongattack}, adversarial FR surveys
catalog multiple attack and defense families \cite{frsurvey}, and OODFace
benchmarks degradation under corruptions and appearance variation
\cite{oodface}. Robust FR therefore requires not only clean identity
classification but also stable embeddings under perturbed or shifted probes.
Existing robustness recipes give useful foundations,
especially robust optimization \cite{madry} and ensemble adversarial training
\cite{tramer}, but face recognition has two additional complications. First,
its decision surface is embedding-centered rather than only closed-set
classification. Second, different margin-based recognizers and different
face-oriented attacks can create different failure modes.

This motivates a training-time robustness objective for a single deployed
recognizer. Attack diversity probes the target from different perturbation
procedures, while recognizer diversity probes the geometry induced by the
identity objective. The cost is higher training pressure, memory use, and
wall-clock time. The benefit, if the ablation supports it, is that robustness
is absorbed into the target rather than shifted into a deployment stack of
separate recognizers, detectors, or attack-specific modules. RobustBench and
MultiRobustBench motivate this caution by showing how robustness can be
overestimated when evaluation is too narrow \cite{robustbench,multirobustbench}.
This project applies the same multi-pressure logic inside FR training and
tests whether the resulting target moves to a better robustness point.

This work asks whether recognizer diversity adds anything once attack diversity
is already present. The intuition is that ArcFace, CosFace, and CurricularFace
shape the embedding space through different margin geometries, so using them as
training-time judges may expose failures that a single ArcFace target would not
see alone. The same pressure could also disturb the clean pairwise geometry
needed for verification. The contribution is a controlled training framework
that separates clean training, recognizer pressure, attack pressure, attack
diversity, and their combination.

\section{Background and Design Rationale}
The proposed training design has three key elements: the deployed target
recognizer, the attacks used to generate hard examples, and the external
recognizer objectives used only during training. The rationale for each part is
different. Attack diversity addresses the risk of overfitting to one threat
model, recognizer diversity addresses the geometry of the face embedding space,
and large-scale FR training determines how much of this can be made practical.

The attacker side of the design follows from prior work on adversarial
robustness. Robust optimization and ensemble adversarial training provide the
foundation \cite{madry,tramer}, while RobustBench and MultiRobustBench show at
benchmark scale that single-attack evaluation can overestimate practical
robustness \cite{robustbench,multirobustbench}. FR-specific work provides the
corresponding domain evidence: decision-based black-box attacks have been
shown against real recognition systems \cite{dongattack}, surveys document
multiple perceptible and imperceptible attack families \cite{frsurvey}, and
OODFace extends reliability evaluation to corruptions and appearance variation
\cite{oodface}.

The recognizer side comes from the structure of modern FR itself. ArcFace,
CosFace, and CurricularFace all use normalized embeddings and margin-based
classification, but they bias training differently: ArcFace enforces an
additive angular margin, CosFace uses a cosine-margin formulation, and
CurricularFace changes the treatment of hard examples during training
\cite{arcface,cosface,curricularface}. They form a practical recognizer set
because they are strong objectives with different decision geometries on the
same embedding space. The central design question is whether attacker diversity
should be paired with recognizer-objective diversity when hardening one
deployed target.

Large-scale FR also constrains what can be tested. WebFace260M and
WebFace42M/WebFace4M motivate million-image identity training
\cite{webface260m}, while Partial FC makes the large-class margin head
trainable by sampling classifier weights \cite{partialfc}. The present
experiments therefore test ensemble pressure in a large-identity regime where
classifier-head memory, attack generation, and auxiliary recognizer losses all
compete for GPU memory.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Training Objective}
The defended target is an ArcFace model with an iResNet100 backbone and a
512-dimensional embedding. Training uses WebFace4M manifests with
RetinaFace-style aligned $112{\times}112$ crops. The experiments use a
205,849-identity WebFace4M subset obtained with a fixed random seed and a
minimum of two images per identity.

The loss is constructed to make the source of pressure explicit. It combines
clean classification, adversarial classification, embedding-consistency,
optional recognizer-ensemble pressure, and hard-sample pressure:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} =
\lambda_c\mathcal{L}_{c} +
\lambda_a\mathcal{L}_{a} +
\lambda_r\mathcal{L}_{r} +
\lambda_s\mathcal{L}_{sim} +
\lambda_h\mathcal{L}_{hard}.
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal{L}_{c}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{a}$ keep the target discriminative on
clean and adversarial images, $\mathcal{L}_{r}$ asks external recognizer heads
to judge the same representations, $\mathcal{L}_{sim}$ discourages excessive
clean/adversarial embedding drift, and $\mathcal{L}_{hard}$ emphasizes mined
hard pairs or hard samples. The recognizer ensemble is used only as a
training-time stressor; inference uses the single target recognizer.

\subsection{Recognizer and Attacker Ensembles}
The recognizer ensemble contains ArcFace, CosFace, and CurricularFace heads,
three strong and widely used margin-loss variants. The attacker suite contains
PGD, BPFA, and DFANet. PGD is the white-box optimization anchor, BPFA adds
transfer-oriented pressure, and DFANet adds FR-specific feature-level
pressure. This suite does not cover every modern attack; it provides a
controlled test of whether different attacker and recognizer pressures improve
a single target.

Training uses a three-stage curriculum: clean warmup, shallow adversarial
pressure, then full adversarial pressure. In the main 24-epoch schedule, clean
warmup lasts 3 epochs, shallow adversarial training lasts 9 epochs, and full
adversarial training lasts 12 epochs. The shallow and full phases use the same
attack families but not the same attack strength: shallow training uses reduced
budgets and restart depth so that adversarial pressure is introduced before
the strongest inner loops dominate optimization, while the final phase uses
the full primary-attacker rotation. The full condition uses a 50\% clean /
50\% adversarial batch split with one primary attacker family active per
adversarial batch. Adversarial intent is also balanced between impersonation
and dodging, matching the two main failure modes of face-recognition systems.

\subsection{Practical Training Pipeline}
Each iteration is arranged around a deployable target branch and training-only
pressure branches. Clean images always update the ArcFace/iResNet100 target
through the margin objective. During adversarial stages, a fixed portion of
the batch is copied into an attack branch, where one attacker family is chosen
in round-robin order and optimized against the current target/surrogate state.
The online attacks use random starts and restart selection where configured,
so the adversarial branch is not only a deterministic augmentation path. The
generated adversarial images then re-enter the same target branch for
adversarial classification and clean/adversarial embedding consistency. This
keeps the model being optimized identical to the model being evaluated.

Recognizer pressure is attached after the target representation has been
formed rather than by deploying multiple recognizers. The ArcFace, CosFace, and
CurricularFace recognizer objectives evaluate the same clean or adversarial
samples and contribute auxiliary losses during training, with weighted
recognizer terms to prevent a single head from dominating. The target Partial
FC rate is 0.30, while auxiliary recognizer heads use a lower sampling rate of
0.12 to avoid turning the recognizer ensemble into a memory bottleneck. The
attacker ensemble is similarly implemented as scheduled pressure rather than
as one combined adversarial batch: PGD, BPFA, and DFANet are rotated across
adversarial batches, while attack generation itself is chunked to fit the
large iResNet100 target and Partial FC classifier into 24 GB GPUs. The hard
sample term is also staged, so early epochs first learn identity structure
before harder mined pairs receive substantial weight. The final trained model
remains one target recognizer; the ensemble exists only in the training graph.

\subsection{Comparison Sequence}
The comparison sequence isolates one source of pressure at a time.
\emph{Baseline-clean}
sets the non-robust recognition reference. \emph{Recognizer-ensemble-only}
tests whether recognizer pressure alone changes clean embedding quality.
\emph{Single-attack-only} tests the risk of narrow adversarial training.
\emph{Attack-ensemble-only} tests the conventional multiattack improvement.
\emph{Full-ensemble} then adds recognizer diversity on top of the same
attacker suite, making it the central comparison for the main claim.

\subsection{Threat Model and Evaluation Boundary}
The evaluated attacks are digital image-space attacks, not physical or
presentation attacks. PGD is treated as a white-box optimization attack, BPFA
as transfer-oriented pressure, and DFANet as an FR feature-level attack. During
training, attacks are generated against the current target/surrogate state, so
the setup is not defense by obscurity. Robust classification is
identification-style: it asks whether the closed-set identity prediction
survives perturbation. Pair verification instead asks whether embeddings still
separate same-identity from different-identity pairs.

The evaluation is not adaptive. It does not test an adversary that optimizes
through the complete training-time recognizer ensemble, tunes attacks after
observing all defense hyperparameters, or performs query-based score/decision
attacks against a deployed system. The claim is therefore restricted to the
PGD/BPFA/DFANet digital suite and the ablation question of whether
recognizer-objective pressure adds robustness once attacker diversity is
already present.

\section{Experimental Setup and Metrics}
All main experiments use ArcFace/iResNet100 on WebFace4M with 4,015,275
training images and 219,826 validation images over 205,849 identities. Partial
FC uses a 0.30 negative sampling rate to keep large-class training feasible on
8 RTX 4090D GPUs. Main per-GPU batch settings are clean warmup 128, shallow
adversarial 48, full adversarial 32, and attack chunk size 32. Mixed precision,
channels-last tensors, distributed training, and manifest-backed dataloading
are used.

Metrics are split by the question they answer. Closed-set validation measures
identity classification; robust validation measures adversarial accuracy, ASR,
clean-to-robust drop, and per-attack behavior; verification measures pairwise
accuracy, EER, ROC-AUC, TAR at low FAR, and cosine separation. Verification is
in-domain: pairs are sampled from the WebFace4M training manifest and
validation identities overlap training identities, so it is an
embedding-geometry analysis rather than an external generalization claim. It
deduplicates pair images, extracts embeddings in distributed GPU batches,
scores cosine similarities in chunks, and computes ROC statistics from sorted
scores. Training cost is reported with wall time, GPU-hours, throughput,
attack-generation share, peak CUDA memory, and active per-GPU batch sizes;
inference cost is unchanged because the auxiliary recognizers and attackers
are discarded after training.

Several implementation choices were made specifically to keep the full setting
trainable rather than merely conceptually defined. The recognizer pressure is
stage-gated so the clean warmup remains fast; auxiliary recognizer heads use a
lower Partial FC sampling rate than the target; attack batches are chunked;
verification is batched and distributed; and the full-adversarial batch size is
kept at 32 after larger batch trials increased memory pressure without improving
stable throughput. Training attackers and evaluation attackers are kept
separate in configuration: clean-only and recognizer-only conditions receive no
adversarial training pressure, but the final robustness table evaluates all
conditions against the same PGD/BPFA/DFANet suite.

The evaluation suite is narrower than the future threat model. Stronger
AutoAttack/APGD-style checks, TransMix-style transfer attacks, AdvFaceGAN,
Adv-Makeup, Greedy-DiM morphing, diffusion-based attacks, and physical or
PAD-aware stress tests are not counted as evidence here because they require
faithful separate implementations or generated-image protocols
\cite{advfacegan,advmakeup,greedydim}.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Overview}
The results support a conditional claim. Recognizer pressure alone preserves
clean in-domain verification but does not make the target robust. Narrow
PGD-only pressure is unstable and damages both classification and verification.
The full method becomes useful after the attacker side is already diversified:
ArcFace/CosFace/CurricularFace pressure improves robustness but moves the
embedding space away from the clean-verification optimum.

Table \ref{tab:robust} answers the primary robustness question by holding the
attacker suite fixed. Moving from attack-ensemble-only to full-ensemble raises
robust accuracy from 52.32\% to 56.55\%, lowers ASR from 44.32\% to 39.45\%,
and reduces the clean-to-robust drop from 41.65 to 36.85 points. The controlled
difference between two rows that share PGD, BPFA, and DFANet is the empirical
signal for recognizer-objective diversity.

The added full-suite evaluations sharpen the lower end of the comparison
sequence. Clean and recognizer-only models are almost completely vulnerable under the
three-attack suite, with robust accuracy near 0.2000\% and ASR near 99.80\%. The
recognizer-only condition therefore improves verification slightly but does not
create adversarial robustness by itself. Single-attack training is a partial
case: the three-attack average is 34.51\%, with BPFA at 45.71\% but
PGD and DFANet both near 28.90\%. That pattern suggests PGD-only training does
not transfer evenly across attack families.

The full-versus-attack-ensemble per-attack structure is more informative than
the average. PGD improves from 45.45\% to 52.55\%, BPFA from 66.09\% to
69.56\%, and DFANet from 45.43\% to 47.55\%. The largest gain on PGD suggests
that recognizer pressure helps the target resist direct margin-optimization
rather than transfer-style noise. BPFA remains the easiest attack after
training, consistent with transfer pressure being partly absorbed by the
diversified attacker ensemble. DFANet improves least, which is consistent with
feature-level FR attacks aligning more closely with the embedding objective
being optimized.

The clean-accuracy movement complicates the robustness gain. Full-ensemble
clean accuracy is slightly lower than attack-ensemble-only (93.40\% versus
93.98\%), so the recognizer heads do not behave like clean regularizers. They
appear to reshape the representation toward perturbation stability. The
single-attack row is more severe: PGD-only training reaches 79.69\% clean
accuracy and 34.51\% average robust accuracy, consistent with
over-specialized adversarial pressure degrading the margin classifier while
leaving substantial cross-attack vulnerability.

Table \ref{tab:verif} is an embedding-geometry analysis rather than an external
generalization benchmark. The verification bundle contains 409,196 WebFace4M
pairs from the training manifest, and validation identities overlap training
identities. Near-ceiling clean verification is expected; the relevant signal is
the relative degradation pattern across rows, especially at low-FAR operating
points and in cosine separation.

The clean and recognizer-only rows provide the control. Both retain about
99.96\% verification accuracy, near-perfect ROC-AUC, and EER below 0.0500\%.
The recognizer-only condition slightly improves EER and low-FAR TAR, so the
external margin heads are not inherently harmful to the embedding space.
Degradation appears when margin diversity is coupled to adversarial image
pressure, where the target must satisfy both clean identity separation and
perturbed-feature stability.

The single-attack row exposes the most severe geometric failure: 89.74\%
verification accuracy, 12.82\% EER, 92.01\% ROC-AUC, and only 0.37\% TAR@1e-4.
That low-FAR collapse indicates a score-distribution shift, not only a loss of
class accuracy. For FR, this makes closed-set robust accuracy insufficient on
its own.

Attack-ensemble training restores much of this structure while providing
robustness: 98.33\% verification accuracy, 1.78\% EER, and 99.75\% ROC-AUC.
The full ensemble then improves robust accuracy at a small but coherent
verification cost: 98.21\% accuracy, 1.93\% EER, and TAR@1e-4 of 84.81\%.
The mean negative cosine increases from 0.0440 to 0.0640, indicating weaker
negative-pair separation. The full model is harder to attack, but its
adversarial invariance appears to compress part of the clean verification
geometry.

The full method is a movement along a robustness--verification frontier. For a
deployment where adversarial probes are plausible, the 4.23-point robust
accuracy gain and 4.87-point ASR reduction may justify the roughly
0.12-point verification-accuracy loss and 0.15-point EER increase relative to
attack-ensemble-only. For a deployment dominated by clean verification, the
clean or recognizer-only rows remain preferable. This distinction is the
study's main interpretive result: multi-recognizer multi-attacker training is
robustness pressure, not a universal replacement for clean FR training.

Table \ref{tab:cost} reports the cost of the strongest implemented pressure
point under a memory-capped batch schedule. The clean baseline takes 12.70
wall-clock hours. Recognizer-only training roughly doubles the cost to 26.10
hours because the auxiliary recognizer heads require additional backbone/head
passes even without attacks. Single-attack training takes 37.60 hours,
attack-ensemble training takes 75.50 hours, and the full condition reaches
110.1 hours, or 880.7 GPU-hours on the 8-GPU setup. Wall time mixes algorithmic
cost with a memory-cap artifact: clean and recognizer-only conditions train at
batch 128, while adversarial phases are capped at 48 and 32. The baseline
therefore uses up to four times the full-adversarial batch size for a
significant amount of training time.

The dominant cost is the adversarial inner loop. Attack-ensemble-only spends
74.90\% of measured train compute on attack generation, and the full ensemble
still spends 67.70\% there despite the added recognizer losses. Each attacked
batch performs repeated target/surrogate forward-backward passes before the
ordinary parameter update; the full condition also evaluates recognizer heads
and samples large-class margin weights. Peak memory reaches 22.60 GB on 24 GB
cards, so the method is limited both by repeated computation and by reduced
batch size. The bottleneck is therefore not the dataloader but repeated
adversarial optimization under a large-class FR head.

The full ensemble gains 4.23 robust-accuracy points over attack-ensemble-only
at the cost of 34.60 additional wall-clock hours and 276.50 GPU-hours. The
marginal robustness gain is therefore expensive. Existing acceleration work
suggests several practical directions: recycling gradients or adversarial
states as in Free Adversarial Training \cite{freeadv}, reducing repeated full
backpropagation as in YOPO \cite{yopo}, or using cheaper single-step
adversaries with safeguards against catastrophic overfitting
\cite{fastadv}. For this FR setting, the closest direct ablations are
lower-frequency recognizer pressure, cached or replayed adversarial seeds,
cheaper early-epoch attack curricula, shared surrogate forwards across attacks,
and selective full-depth attack refreshes. These target the measured bottleneck
without departing from the multi-recognizer multi-attacker design objective.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
The work is strongest as an ablation-backed framework result. It does not show
state-of-the-art FR, that every ensemble member is beneficial, or that the
selected attacks are sufficient for deployment. It shows a narrower result:
when a target already receives multiattack pressure, adding
recognizer-objective diversity improves measured robustness under the same
attacker suite. The single-attack result also suggests that narrow PGD-only
training is weaker than broader attack-family training.

Several limitations remain. First, the attacker suite is explicit but not
exhaustive. Second, verification and closed-set robust classification answer
different questions, and both are needed for FR robustness. Third, the
verification protocol is in-domain; external unseen-identity benchmarks such
as LFW, CFP-FP, AgeDB, or IJB-style evaluations are needed before deployment
claims. Fourth, the recognizer ensemble is training-time pressure, not a
deployment ensemble. Fifth, training cost affects the design itself:
memory-limited batch sizes, repeated attack forwards, and large-class margin
heads make the full condition much slower than the clean baseline. Finally,
the work does not yet establish robustness to adaptive ensemble attacks,
modern semantic or generative attacks, morphing attacks, corruptions, OOD
appearance variation, or physical/PAD-aware attacks.

\section{Conclusion}
This study implements a face-recognition robustness framework that trains one
ArcFace/iResNet100 target under explicit recognizer and attacker ensemble
pressure. The result is an ablation-backed tradeoff rather than a simple
leaderboard win. Recognizer-objective diversity adds measured adversarial
robustness beyond attacker diversity, but the gain comes with lower
clean-verification performance and a large training-cost increase. The method
is therefore best understood as a practical training framework for FR
hardening: useful when digital adversarial robustness is part of the operating
requirement, less attractive when clean verification is the only priority, and
still bounded by the attacks and in-domain verification protocol evaluated
here.
